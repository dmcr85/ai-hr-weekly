From 1 October, Connecticut Makes “Was It the AI?” a Question Every Mass Layoff Has to Answer in Writing
BREAKING Regulation HR Tech
Connecticut’s SB 5 takes effect on 1 October 2026, and one provision inside it is a genuine first. Any Connecticut employer filing a WARN notice with the state Department of Labour must disclose whether the layoffs were caused or contributed to by the employer’s use of artificial intelligence or other technological change. It binds employers with 100 or more employees — the existing WARN threshold — and triggers on the usual qualifying event of 50 or more job losses inside 90 days. The Attorney General enforces it as an unfair or deceptive trade practice, which means an evasive or unsupportable answer is its own exposure, separate from the layoff itself. Note that this is a standalone duty: the bill’s automated employment decision technology provisions do not commence until 1 October 2027, so nobody gets to bundle the two into one 2027 programme. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: every other instrument this digest has tracked asks an employer to document a process. This one asks an employer to attribute causation, under penalty, about a decision that was made months earlier by people who have since moved on — and no HR estate in the country records it. HRSD holds the case, the lifecycle event, the position record and the termination reason, but there is no field anywhere in the standard data model that says “this role was eliminated because a system took the work.” Reconstructing that after an AG letter arrives means interviewing the transformation programme, and the answer will be contested internally before it is ever contested externally. The buildable artefact is a technology-causation record captured at decision time: when a role is eliminated, the workflow asks whether automation, AI or a technology change contributed, requires the named accountable executive to attest, and links the answer to the specific programme or system. It is a small configuration change with a fifteen-day runway and an Attorney General attached, which makes it the easiest compliance conversation you will have this quarter.

The No Robo Bosses Act Is on Newsom’s Desk With Fourteen Days to Run — and It Wants a Verification, Not an Approval
HOT Regulation HRSD
California SB 947, the No Robo Bosses Act, passed the Senate 28–10 and the Assembly 53–14 in late August, and Governor Newsom must sign or veto by 30 September. It is Senator McNerney’s reintroduction of SB 7, which Newsom vetoed in October 2025, revised to address his stated objections. If enacted on 1 July 2027 it would bar employers from relying solely on an automated decision system to fire or discipline a worker, require human oversight and independent verification before those decisions, prohibit the use of ADS for predictive behaviour analysis of workers, require post-use notice when ADS informed a termination or disciplinary decision, and extend all of it to independent contractors as well as employees. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: read the words “independent verification” carefully, because they are not satisfied by the approval step you already have. An approval records that a human clicked; a verification records that a human looked at evidence independent of the system’s own output and could have reached a different conclusion. In HRSD terms that is a different record with different fields — what the reviewer was shown, what they consulted outside the recommendation, how long they held it, and what the outcome was when they disagreed. If your disagreement rate is zero across a year, you do not have a verification control, you have a rubber stamp with a timestamp, and that will be the first statistic anyone asks for. The second sleeper is the predictive behaviour analysis prohibition. Flight-risk scoring, attrition prediction and engagement-signal models are standard in workforce analytics and most clients have never classified them as ADS at all, let alone asked whether predicting a worker’s future conduct is the thing this bill forbids. That scoping question is worth putting to every Californian client now, while the answer is still cheap to act on.

Mobley v. Workday Survived Dismissal on an Agent Theory — the HR Tech Vendor Now Shares the Discrimination Exposure
WATCH Litigation HR Tech
On 22 June 2026 Judge Rita Lin in the Northern District of California declined to dismiss most of the claims in Mobley v. Workday, including state-law bias claims that reach the software vendor itself under an agent theory — the AI tool acting on the employer’s behalf. Derek Mobley, the lead plaintiff, says he applied for more than 100 roles through employers using Workday’s screening tools and was rejected every time; he is Black, over 40, and lives with anxiety and depression. Judge Lin also held that because the tools were designed and maintained in California, Californian civil rights law may reach applicants who applied from other states, which is a considerably larger footprint than the defendant would like. The load-bearing finding is the refusal to accept that a technology provider is insulated simply because it does not make the final hiring decision. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: this changes the shape of the vendor conversation from procurement to joint liability, and it cuts both ways in a useful manner. A client can no longer point at the vendor’s model and call the ranking someone else’s problem; the vendor can no longer point at the client’s hiring manager and call the outcome someone else’s problem. What survives that argument is evidence about configuration, and almost nobody keeps it. The artefact worth building is a provenance record for every surface in the HR estate that scores, ranks or filters a person: which model and version, who set the thresholds and when, what the tuning changed, who approved it, and which outcomes followed. It is the same evidentiary spine the EU Annex IV work needs and the same one SB 947’s verification requirement needs. Third time this quarter the same record has answered a different question — that is the sign to build it once, properly, rather than three times badly.

NSW’s Digital Work Systems Duties Still Have Not Commenced — Seven Months On, the Date Is Still a Proclamation Away
WATCH Australia Regulation
The Work Health and Safety Amendment (Digital Work Systems) Act 2026 passed NSW Parliament on 12 February 2026, making NSW the first Australian jurisdiction to regulate the work health and safety hazards of algorithmic management. It defines a digital work system broadly — “an algorithm, artificial intelligence, automation or online platform” — and requires employers to ensure that its use does not put workers’ health and safety at risk, covering AI-driven work allocation, performance monitoring, automated rostering and algorithmic management generally. As at 8 September the principal duties had still not commenced: commencement is by proclamation and only selected supporting amendments began on assent. The Australian Industry Group has continued to describe the Act as fundamentally flawed and unwarranted, so the delay is political rather than administrative. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: this is the instrument closest to home and the only one on this page whose start date is genuinely unknown, which makes it a planning problem rather than a compliance problem. Two things are worth saying plainly to Australian clients. First, this is a WHS duty, not a privacy duty — it is enforced by SafeWork NSW, it attaches to officers personally under the due diligence obligation, and it is a completely different risk conversation from the Privacy Act one most HR teams have already had. Second, a WHS duty is discharged through hazard identification and control, so the preparation is not a policy document but a hazard pass over the algorithmic management surfaces already live in HRSD: rostering, work allocation, case-load balancing, productivity monitoring and any agent that sets the pace of someone’s work. That pass has value whether the proclamation lands in November or next July, and it is the rare compliance exercise that produces an operational finding worth having anyway.

HOT Governance

Brazil’s Senate Takes the Final Floor Vote on an EU-Shaped AI Act Today
BREAKING Regulation
The Brazilian Federal Senate scheduled the final plenary floor vote on Bill 2338/2023 for 16 September 2026 — today. The bill establishes a comprehensive legal framework for artificial intelligence whose structure is heavily influenced by the EU AI Act, meaning a risk-tier architecture with elevated obligations for high-risk uses. In the same window the United Kingdom’s AI Regulation and Safety Bill advances to House of Lords committee stage on 22 September, and the US Department of Commerce has been engaging Ghana’s National Communications Authority on oversight design. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: the significant fact is not that Brazil is regulating, it is that Brazil is regulating in the same shape. Every jurisdiction that adopts the EU’s risk-tier structure makes the Annex-III-style classification pass more reusable and the per-jurisdiction compliance project less defensible. If a client with Brazilian operations has already scoped EU work — the classification of AI touchpoints in the HR estate, the technical file, the human-oversight evidence — most of it transfers, and the marginal cost of covering a second jurisdiction is the mapping rather than the artefact. That is a genuinely good commercial story to tell a multinational who has been treating the EU programme as a sunk regional cost. It is also worth flagging the converse honestly: convergence on structure does not mean convergence on thresholds, definitions or timelines, and a client who assumes the EU dossier is automatically sufficient in São Paulo will be wrong in the details that matter. Sell the reuse, scope the delta.

The Technology-Causation Record — Make “Was It the AI?” Answerable Before the First WARN Filing Lands
From 1 October, any Connecticut employer with 100 or more staff filing a WARN notice must state whether artificial intelligence or another technological change caused or contributed to the layoffs, with the Attorney General enforcing a bad answer as an unfair or deceptive trade practice. Every other AI-employment instrument asks an employer to document a process. This one asks an employer to attribute causation, in writing, about decisions taken months earlier — and there is no field in the standard HR data model that records it. Build the record that makes the answer defensible, captured at decision time rather than reconstructed under a regulator’s letter. Extend the workforce-change workflow in HRSD so that any position elimination, role redesign or restructure event requires a technology-contribution determination before it can close: a structured classification (no contribution, enabling contribution, direct substitution), a mandatory link to the specific programme, system or agent where one is claimed, the evidence relied on, and an attestation from the named accountable executive rather than from HR operations. Wire it to the position record and the lifecycle event so the attribution survives the departure of everyone who made the decision. Then build the aggregation layer that matters at filing time: given a WARN-qualifying event — 50 or more separations inside 90 days — assemble every contributing determination, surface the inconsistencies between them before the regulator does, and produce a filing-ready statement with its evidence trail attached. Add a drift report so a client can see, quarterly, how much of their workforce reduction is being attributed to technology, because that trend line is a board conversation whether or not Connecticut asks for it. Deliver the scoped configuration, the determination taxonomy with worked examples, the filing assembly report, a decision-rights map naming who attests, and a short briefing for the general counsel on the difference between a defensible attribution and a guess. Flagship because the deadline is fifteen days away, because the configuration is small relative to the exposure, and because the artefact generalises — the same causation record answers the Connecticut question this year and the redundancy-consultation and works-council questions that follow it everywhere else.

The Verification Record — Build What SB 947 Actually Asks For, Which Is Not the Approval You Already Have
California’s No Robo Bosses Act sits on the Governor’s desk with a 30 September decision and a 1 July 2027 operative date, and its central requirement is human oversight with independent verification before an automated system contributes to a termination or disciplinary decision. Approval and verification are different records and most HR estates only hold the first. An approval proves a human clicked; a verification proves a human considered evidence independent of the system’s own recommendation and was in a position to reach a different conclusion. Build the second one. Instrument the disciplinary and termination workflow so that where an automated system contributed at any point — case scoring, policy matching, evidence summarisation, pattern detection across cases — the reviewer step captures what they were shown, what independent evidence they consulted, what the system recommended, what they decided, and where the two diverged. Make divergence a first-class field rather than an exception, because the single most revealing statistic about any human-in-the-loop control is the rate at which the human disagrees, and a twelve-month disagreement rate of zero is evidence against the control rather than for it. Then run the analysis that tells the client the truth: sample real reviewed decisions, measure median review duration against decision complexity, and establish whether reviewers are verifying or rubber-stamping. Pair it with a scoping pass on predictive behaviour analysis, which the bill prohibits outright — flight-risk scores, attrition models and engagement-signal analytics are standard in workforce analytics and most clients have never classified them as automated decision systems at all, which makes this the cheapest high-value question in the engagement. Deliver the verification record configuration, the divergence-rate dashboard, a rubber-stamp diagnostic with findings from real data, the predictive-analytics classification register, and a post-use notice template. Flagship because the same record satisfies EU Article 14 human-oversight evidence and the configuration provenance the Mobley agent theory now makes discoverable — one artefact, three regulators, and a finding the client cannot get from a policy review.

Brazil reached Early Availability in mid-September with November general availability, and the September store release gives ServiceNow Fluent domain separation support on records and APIs, including setting sys_domain and using sys_override so generated applications behave correctly in a domain-separated instance. For most of the platform this is a footnote. Technology-Causation Record for Workforce Reductions

Idea

Capture whether AI or automation contributed to a role elimination at the moment the decision is made — because from 1 October a Connecticut WARN filing has to say so, under the Attorney General’s enforcement.

Intermediate ServiceNow HRSD · Workforce Events · Scoped App New Added 16 Sep 2026

Hypothesis

Technology causation for a workforce reduction cannot be reconstructed reliably after the fact — the people who made the decision have moved on, the transformation programme and HR will disagree, and the disagreement is discoverable. Captured as a structured determination at decision time inside the HRSD workforce-change workflow, it costs almost nothing and produces a filing-ready answer that a general counsel will sign.

Approach

  1. Extend the position elimination, role redesign and restructure workflows so a technology-contribution determination is mandatory before the event can close, using a three-level taxonomy: no contribution, enabling contribution, direct substitution.
  2. Require a link to the specific programme, system or agent wherever a contribution is claimed, plus the evidence relied on and an attestation from the named accountable executive rather than from HR operations.
  3. Bind the determination to the position record and the lifecycle event so the attribution survives the departure of every person involved in making it.
  4. Build the filing assembly report: given a qualifying event of 50 or more separations inside 90 days, gather every contributing determination, surface inconsistencies between them before a regulator does, and output a filing-ready statement with its evidence trail.
  5. Add a quarterly drift report showing what proportion of workforce reduction is being attributed to technology, and test the whole thing against a reconstructed prior-year reduction to measure how much worse the retrospective answer is.

Deliverables

Scoped configuration for the determination and attestation; determination taxonomy with worked examples and edge cases; filing assembly report; decision-rights map naming who attests and who may not; general counsel briefing on the difference between a defensible attribution and a guess.

Client fit

Any client with Connecticut headcount above the 100-employee WARN threshold, and more broadly any multinational running technology-driven workforce change — the same record answers redundancy consultation, works council and EU collective dismissal questions, and the board-level question of how much reduction is being attributed to automation.

Effort & prerequisites

4–6 days, one HRSD practitioner plus employment counsel review. Requires an HRSD sandbox with workforce or position management configured and access to a representative set of prior reduction events for the retrospective test.

Success signal

A filing-ready statement generated from live records that employment counsel accepts without rework, and a measurable gap between the contemporaneous determination and the reconstructed one that proves the point to the client in their own data.

Annex IV Dossier Generator for HR Screening Systems

Idea

Generate the Article 11 technical file an EU inspector actually asks for — from live HRSD configuration rather than from a consultant-written Word document.

Advanced ServiceNow HRSD · Recruiting · AI Control Tower Added 11 Sep 2026

Hypothesis

Most of the Article 11 and Annex IV technical file for an HR screening system already exists inside the client’s HRSD instance as configuration — flow and integration topology, approval and override settings, ACLs and User Criteria, case outcome data — and a dossier generated from that live configuration on a schedule is both cheaper to produce and materially more defensible under inspection than a point-in-time document written by hand.

Approach

  1. Build a classification pass: a scoped app that enumerates every AI touchpoint in the HR estate — Now Assist skills, AI agents, Applicant Center and Recruitment Workspace capability, AI-assisted requisition drafting, candidate matching, and any skill that ranks or scores a person — and classifies each against Annex III.
  2. Generate the Article 11 system architecture section from flow, integration and MCP records, with model provider, version and serving region attached to each inference point.
  3. Generate the Article 10 training-data governance section with an explicit deployer/provider split, naming precisely which evidence the vendor must supply and which the client owns.
  4. Generate the Article 14 human-oversight section from actual approval, override and escalation configuration plus a tested halt mechanism — not from policy prose.
  5. Generate the Article 9 risk register and the Article 15 accuracy and bias section from real case outcome data, disaggregated across protected groups where the client lawfully holds it.
  6. Schedule regeneration and drift detection so the dossier stays current after the first pass, and produce a provider-obligation letter template for the vendor-held sections.

Deliverables

Clients with high-investment custom intranets (SharePoint, Unily, bespoke) will adopt EmployeeWorks faster if they can embed it without fronting the Employee Center portal.

Approach

  1. Scaffold a minimal React SPA mimicking a client intranet shell.
  2. Call the EmployeeWorks Headless API for conversation, authentication, and handoff.
  3. Implement three flows: policy query, case creation, handoff-to-human.
  4. Benchmark response time and conversational fidelity vs. the native Employee Center chat.

Deliverables

Working SPA, API integration reference code, performance benchmark, integration pattern document.

Client fit

Enterprise clients with existing intranet platforms ruling out a portal-first rollout.

Effort & prerequisites

7–9 days, one practitioner with front-end skills.

Success signal

All three flows functional, response parity within 150 ms of native channel, reusable integration module ready.
