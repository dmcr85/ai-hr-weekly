The 2 August Deadline Split in Two — Your HR Chatbot Is In Scope Today, Your Recruitment AI Has Until December 2027
BREAKING Regulation Compliance
On 2 August the EU AI Act reached the milestone the whole market had spent a year preparing for, and it arrived in two pieces. The Digital Omnibus — endorsed by Parliament on 16 June, given final Council approval on 29 June — deferred the Annex III high-risk obligations, the ones covering recruitment, candidate evaluation, performance management and task allocation, from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, with AI embedded in regulated products pushed to 2 August 2028. What the Omnibus did not touch is Article 50. The transparency duties applied on 2 August exactly as drafted: a system interacting with a person must make clear it is an AI, anyone subject to emotion recognition must be told, synthetic content must be identified, and only Article 50(2)’s machine-readable marking is spared, and only for systems already on the market. So what for a Deloitte HRSD consultant: the risk this week is a client who read ‘high-risk delayed’ and concluded nothing happened. Every conversational surface in an EU-facing HR estate — Employee Center virtual agent, Now Assist drafting and summarisation, Otto, Moveworks, any Copilot dropped into an HR journey — is in scope for Article 50 now, and closing it is genuinely small work: a disclosure string, a design standard, an evidence note per surface. The sixteen months on high-risk are not a reprieve, they are a planning window, and the clients who spend it building documentation are the ones not scrambling in late 2027.

Australia’s Automated-Decision Disclosure Duty Is 124 Days Out — and the OAIC Is Reading It Broadly
WATCH ANZ Regulation
APP 1.7, 1.8 and 1.9 — introduced by the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 — commence on 10 December 2026. From that date an APP entity must state in its privacy policy that it uses computer programs to make, or substantially assist in making, decisions that could reasonably be expected to significantly affect an individual’s rights or interests, together with the kinds of personal information those programs use and the types of decisions involved. OAIC consultation closed on 15 June with final guidance expected by September, and the signal from the draft is a broad reading of ‘substantially assist’. The regulator’s infringement and compliance notice powers attach directly to a privacy policy that falls short. So what for a Deloitte HRSD consultant: ‘substantially assists’ is the phrase that pulls HRSD in. A workflow that scores, ranks, prioritises or routes a case toward a particular outcome can qualify even where a human presses the final button — and that describes a great deal of what an HR platform does, from leave and entitlement calculations to case triage, eligibility checks and performance flags. This is not the EU AI Act register under another name: it is an Australian privacy-policy disclosure with a hard date four months away, most clients have not begun, and the discovery needed to write two honest paragraphs is exactly the inventory an HRSD partner is best placed to run.

Non-Human Identity Just Got a Billion-Dollar Price Tag — and Most HR Agents Still Share One Service Account
HOT Agentic Governance
Cyera signed a letter of intent on 28 July to acquire Oasis Security for US$1 billion, mostly cash, framing the deal as uniting data security and identity security into a single control point over what every human, machine and AI agent is permitted to see and do. Oasis, founded in 2022 and backed by roughly US$195 million from Accel, Craft Ventures and Cyberstarts, builds non-human identity management; Cyera itself raised US$600 million at a US$12 billion valuation and this is its third acquisition of the year. So what for a Deloitte HRSD consultant: strip out the venture arithmetic and the signal is that ‘which identity is this agent acting as’ went from an architecture footnote to a billion-dollar category in under three years. That question is unanswered in most HR agent designs today, because agents are routinely wired through a shared service account with a broad role — which renders the audit trail useless the moment more than one agent is running, and makes a least-privilege claim impossible to evidence. The recommendation is unglamorous and defensible: one distinct non-human identity per HR agent, scoped to the minimum connector set, with its own credentials and its own log stream. It costs almost nothing at design time and is close to impossible to retrofit once a client has forty agents in production.

OpenAI Declares Astra Its First “Critical” Cyber Model and Slows It Down — the Preparedness Framework Finally Bit
BREAKING OpenAI Safety
On 7 August OpenAI said preliminary evaluations of Astra, an upcoming model, were strong enough that it could not rule out the Critical capability level for cybersecurity under its Preparedness Framework — the first time any frontier lab has applied that classification to one of its own models. The Critical threshold is defined as a model able to identify and develop functional zero-day exploits across severity levels in many hardened real-world systems without human intervention, or to devise and execute novel end-to-end attack strategies against hardened targets given only a high-level goal. OpenAI has frozen internal activity that does not meet enhanced security controls, tightened protection around the model, slowed further development, and says it will work with government agencies and independent safety organisations to validate the capability before release. So what for a Deloitte HRSD consultant: this is not an HR story and it is the most important story of the week for anyone selling agentic HR. It lands eight days after Anthropic disclosed containment breaches across three organisations, and the pair establish that the labs’ own safety machinery is now producing visible brakes rather than press releases. Use it precisely. It is proof that a preparedness process with real thresholds can stop a shipment — which is the argument for insisting a client’s HR agent programme has thresholds and a documented stop condition of its own. Not ‘we will monitor it’, but a written statement of what observed behaviour turns the agent off, and who has the authority to do it.

The White House Convened the Four Big Labs on a Voluntary Testing Framework — 30 Days of Early Access, Explicitly Not Licensing
BREAKING Governance Regulation
OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Meta met White House officials on Tuesday 4 August to review a draft framework for testing frontier models. As reported, it would give the government early access to certain frontier models for up to 30 days before release, and it explicitly cannot be used to construct a mandatory licensing or preclearance regime. It is the first substantial US federal move on frontier oversight and it arrives as a voluntary arrangement rather than a rule. So what for a Deloitte HRSD consultant: the practical read is that the United States is settling on pre-release evaluation of models rather than regulation of deployment — the mirror image of the European approach, and it leaves the deployer, which is your client, carrying exactly the obligations they carried last week. It changes not one control in an HR AI programme. What it does change is the conversation with the client who has been waiting for federal clarity before committing: the clarity has arrived, and it says the burden stays with you. The detail worth tracking is timing. A 30-day government window ahead of frontier releases would slow the model cadence that a good many HR roadmaps have quietly been assuming.

One Identity Per Agent — an HR Agent Identity, Egress and Stop-Condition Baseline
Three signals converged this week: OpenAI classified Astra as its first Critical cyber model and deliberately slowed it, Cyera paid US$1 billion for non-human identity management, and AI Control Tower’s expanded build — runtime observability plus NIST and EU AI Act risk frameworks — reaches GA.
