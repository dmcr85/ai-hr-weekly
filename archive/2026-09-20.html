Eighty-One Days to Australia’s Automated-Decision Disclosure Duty — and the Regulator’s Guidance Is Overdue
BREAKING Australia Regulation
From 10 December 2026, Australian Privacy Principle 1.8 requires any APP entity that has arranged for a computer program to make a decision about a person, or to do something substantially and directly related to making it, where that decision could reasonably be expected to significantly affect their rights or interests, to describe it in its privacy policy: the kinds of personal information used, the kinds of decisions made solely by the program, and the kinds where the program is a key factor in a human decision. The OAIC’s Issues Paper of 18 May closed for submissions on 15 June and the regulator said final guidance would land by September; as of this issue we could find none published, and the Issues Paper remains the clearest statement of how it is thinking. Two things in the fine print matter for HR: “computer program” is deliberately broader than AI — the OAIC’s own worked example is a spreadsheet formula — and a rubber-stamp human does not take you out of scope, because the obligation was drafted to catch programs that recommend or guide a decision. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: an HRSD instance is a factory of exactly these programs. Auto-routing and assignment rules, Issue Auto Resolution, sensitivity detection, Otto skills that draft or summarise a case, Recruiter Workspace ranking, any flow that auto-declines a request — each is a candidate for the register a client has to be able to describe in categories by December. The writing job is small; the discovery job is the engagement, and the OAIC has already signalled that generic wording will not do. Note the limit honestly: this is a disclosure duty, not a right to contest or to human review, unlike Principle 10 of WA’s PRIS Act which already requires both for WA public sector entities since 1 July.

Bersin’s Galileo Jupiter Turns Thirty Years of HR Research Into an MCP Server — and Points It at Your HRSD Case Data
NEW HR Tech Agentic
The Josh Bersin Company shipped the Jupiter release of Galileo on 15 September, re-engineering its corpus — nine million words of research, 1,750 client stories, no open-web content — into what it calls an Agent Ready Corpus, exposed through an MCP server and through named “Galileo Inside” integrations for Microsoft Copilot, Workday, HiBob, Gloat and ServiceNow. HiBob followed on 16 September with Galileo-powered agents for job architecture, compensation and performance inside Bob, expected in hundreds of customers by year end. The ServiceNow integration is described as reading HR case and workflow data to spot recurring ticket types, bottlenecked approvals and resurfacing policy questions, then recommending a research-backed fix rather than just resolving the ticket in front of you. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: this is the first credible third-party “intelligence layer” that wants to sit on top of HR case data via MCP rather than replace the platform, and it arrives the same month Astra for Law put a number on what a curated corpus adds. It is also a governance question wearing a product announcement: a vendor agent reading case patterns is a deployer of personal information under the obligation in the story above, and the entitlement leakage we flagged in the MCP User Criteria POC applies to any external MCP client, research-backed or not. Read the Galileo public FAQ before a client asks you what it can see.

California Signed a Ban on AI That Infers a Worker’s Emotional State — Go and Look at What Your Sentiment Skill Does
HOT Regulation HRSD
Buried in the Transparency Coalition’s weekly tally of the twenty-odd AI bills Governor Newsom has signed this month is AB 1883, which prohibits an employer from using a workplace surveillance tool that uses AI to, among other things, collect neural data or identify a worker’s emotional state. Alongside it he signed AB 1405 and SB 813, creating an AI Auditor Registry and standards for independent verification organisations certifying AI compliance, which together make California the first state with a licensed AI-audit profession. Still on the desk with a 30 September deadline: SB 947, the No Robo Bosses Act, and SB 951, which would require 90 days’ notice before technological displacement of a quarter or more of a covered workforce. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: ServiceNow Otto for HRSD has shipped sentiment analysis on HR cases since August 2025. Whether an HR case-management skill that scores the tone of an employee’s message is a “workplace surveillance tool” under AB 1883 is a question employment counsel has to answer, not one to assume either way — but a client with Californian headcount needs an inventory of which skills infer emotional state, what they feed, and whether they can be switched off by jurisdiction. The Auditor Registry is the other one to watch: if an AI audit must be performed by a registered auditor, the assurance work around HR AI stops being something a delivery team signs off on its own.

NEW HR Tech Governance
OpenAI’s Enterprise release notes this fortnight set the timeline for retiring custom GPTs in favour of plugins that combine reusable instructions with connected apps: migration available from 17 September, no new GPT creation from 25 September, retirement on 11 December for affected Enterprise workspaces. In the same window, ChatGPT for Word went generally available on every plan, with Enterprise and Business admins told it will be enabled by default from 1 October unless they turn it off, and the Admin Console gained a task classifier that groups a sample of messages into use cases so admins can see what people actually do with AI. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: every large client has HR policy bots, leave-question GPTs and onboarding assistants built as custom GPTs by people who never talked to the HRSD team. The migration is a forced discovery moment — the list of GPTs to migrate is the list of self-service gaps Employee Center did not close. Word defaulting on from October means HR documents get drafted with a general model unless an admin acts, which lands squarely in the Australian disclosure duty above if any of that drafting shapes a decision about a person. And the task classifier is the adoption evidence clients keep asking Deloitte for; it exists now, so use it in the discovery phase rather than surveying people.

Anthropic’s Life Sciences Verification Program Swaps Real-Time Blocking for Verified Access and Offline Monitoring — the Deployer-Governance Pattern HR Will Inherit
NEW Governance Anthropic
On 17 September Anthropic opened the Life Sciences Verification Program in beta: verified organisations get Mythos, Opus and Sonnet with safeguards more permissive for biology work, under Standard Use grants for whole teams renewed annually and High-risk Use grants scoped to a single project and renewed every six months. The mechanism is the interesting part. Enforcement shifts from blocking each request in real time to offline monitoring of usage against the use cases the organisation declared in its application, with 30-day retention of flagged activity, and organisation admins are given pre-agreed timeframes to triage and remediate when Anthropic flags something. The threat models named are access compromise, insider misuse and agent swarms taking unintended actions. So what for a Deloitte HRSD practitioner: strip out the biology and this is a deployer-accountability framework — declare your use cases, get scoped access, be monitored against your declaration, remediate within an agreed window — and it is structurally the same thing the EU AI Act asks of high-risk HR deployers and the Australian disclosure duty asks in miniature. Expect the same “verified deployer” shape to reach employment-sensitive uses such as investigations and terminations; clients who already run a use-case register will be the ones who qualify first.

The Automated-Decision Register — Generate the APP 1.8 Disclosure From Live HRSD Configuration Before 10 December
Eighty-one days out, the obligation is settled and the guidance is late, so the defensible position is a register built from what the instance actually runs rather than a paragraph written from memory. Build a scoped app that discovers every candidate program — assignment rules and auto-routing flows, Issue Auto Resolution, sensitivity detection, each enabled Otto skill, Recruiter Workspace ranking, any flow with an auto-decline branch — and walks each through the OAIC’s three-limb test with a recorded rationale. For anything in scope, capture where the human sits (advisory or determinative) and pull the actual override rate from case history, because a human who approves everything in four seconds is the example the regulator gave for not being out of scope. Output the “kinds of information / kinds of decisions” wording APP 1.8 asks for, regenerable on schedule, plus a gap list. Why now: the $3M threshold captures every client we have, the same register answers WA Principle 10 and the EU human-oversight annex, and the client who can show a living register in December will be the one the OAIC does not write to first.

The Galileo-Over-MCP Boundary Test — Find Out What a Third-Party Intelligence Layer Can See in an HRSD Sandbox Before a Client Turns It On
Galileo Jupiter is the first serious external corpus that wants to read HRSD case patterns through MCP, and clients will ask us whether it is safe before they ask whether it is useful. Stand up a Zurich sandbox with the HRSD MCP Server Console, connect Galileo’s MCP server (or a stand-in client that mimics its call pattern), seed tiered HR knowledge and Employee Relations cases with distinct User Criteria, and run the same questions as four identities: an employee, a manager, an HR generalist and an ER specialist. Diff what comes back. Then instrument the outbound side: what case text, identifiers and metadata leave the instance per call, whether answers cite the corpus versus the case data, and what the audit trail names as the actor. Produce a reference configuration and a one-page pre-enablement checklist. Why now: it extends the MCP User Criteria POC already in the Lab with the first real vendor that will be on a client’s shortlist, and the answer feeds straight into the automated-decision register above, because a vendor agent shaping a manager’s decision is exactly what APP 1.8 wants disclosed.

The Emotional-State Inventory — Map Which HRSD AI Skills Infer How an Employee Feels, and Make Them Switchable by Jurisdiction
California’s AB 1883 is signed and names “identify a worker’s emotional state” as a prohibited function of an AI workplace surveillance tool; Otto for HRSD has run sentiment analysis on HR cases since August 2025 and sensitivity detection since 2024. Whether those are surveillance tools is a legal question, but a client cannot get counsel’s answer without an inventory first. Build it: enumerate every Otto and Virtual Agent skill on an instance that scores tone, sentiment, sensitivity or wellbeing, document what each feeds (routing, prioritisation, manager dashboards, nothing), and where the output is stored. Then configure the control: skill enablement scoped by the employee’s work location through User Criteria or a dedicated property, tested so a Californian employee’s case is routed without a sentiment score while a Sydney case keeps it. Deliver a jurisdiction matrix, the configuration, and a short counsel briefing that asks the right question rather than answering it. Why now: the statute is enacted, not proposed; the NSW Digital Work Systems Act frames algorithmic management as a WHS hazard using similar language; and it is a beginner-friendly build with an expensive consequence for getting it wrong.
